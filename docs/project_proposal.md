## 1. Overview and Vision

Pre-trained vision-language-action models (VLAs) and world/action models (WAMs) — e.g. Cosmos — already encode broad knowledge about how to perform many everyday manipulation tasks. In practice, however, they fail to execute reliably because they are not tightly conditioned on the specific task and environment at hand. This project investigates whether a **single egocentric human demonstration**, captured passively with Meta ARIA glasses, can serve as that missing conditioning signal at test time.

The long-term vision: a person wears ARIA glasses for a day or two while going about ordinary tasks in their home or workspace. This passive recording is later used, with no additional human effort, to condition a robot policy so that it can perform the same tasks in that same environment. The robot never needs to be taught from scratch; it borrows the human's demonstration as a form of "how to do it here."

## 2. Core Hypothesis

VLAs/WAMs are powerful but are conditioned on inputs that are only loosely connected to the task to be executed. An ARIA recording of a human solving the task is a much more direct, task-specific solution demonstration. Our first hypothesis is:

> **Hypothesis 1.** Conditioning a pre-trained VLA with a single egocentric human demonstration of a task, at test time, significantly increases the policy's success rate on that specific task — analogous to how ControlNet-style image conditioning unlocks latent capability already present in a pre-trained diffusion model, without retraining the base model.

The key differentiator from prior human-to-robot transfer work (e.g. HumanEgo) is that we do **not** train a manipulation policy from scratch on many human demonstrations. Instead we use a large pre-trained VLA and inject a single human demonstration as a conditioning signal at test time, similar in spirit to test-time training approaches recently explored using human play data.

Hypothesis 1 raises an immediate follow-up question: is a human demonstration giving the policy as much conditioning signal as a demonstration *could*, or is there a ceiling above it? A human demonstration must cross an embodiment gap — hand to gripper, human motion to robot kinematics — that a demonstration already living in the robot's own sensorimotor space would not need to cross. This motivates a second hypothesis, which sets that ceiling explicitly:

> **Hypothesis 2.** Conditioning a policy on a demonstration already in the robot's own sensorimotor/action space — including a robot's own past demonstration of the same task — yields a substantially larger success-rate gain than conditioning on a cross-embodiment human demonstration, since there is no representation gap left to bridge between the demonstration and the policy's own action space.

Hypothesis 2 is not merely conjectured: Behavior Prompting Policy (BPP, arXiv 2606.30457) conditions test-time execution on a single demonstration recorded directly in the robot's sensorimotor space (via a handheld iPhUMI interface) and substantially outperforms both goal-image conditioning and ICRT (Section 3.3, which conditions on robot-only demonstration trajectories) on unseen-task generalization — evidence for exactly this pattern, that the closer a test-time demonstration sits to the policy's own action space, the more effectively it conditions the policy. This predicts that Hypothesis 1's human-demonstration results should sit *below* the Hypothesis 2 ceiling, with the gap between them attributable specifically to the cross-embodiment translation cost of using a human demonstration rather than a robot one.

Read together, the two hypotheses define this project's central technical problem: Hypothesis 1 establishes that demonstration-conditioning is worth pursuing at all; Hypothesis 2 establishes the ceiling against which to measure how much of that potential a human demonstration is actually realizing. The task, then, is not simply to make Hypothesis 1 true, but to close the gap between it and Hypothesis 2 — i.e., to **let a human demonstration steer a pretrained video-generation backbone as directly as possible**, so that conditioning approaches as much of Hypothesis 2's effectiveness as possible. This is precisely the role of the video-generation-based conditioning mechanism described in Section 7, and Section 10's evaluation measures this gap directly rather than only reporting Hypothesis 1's improvement in isolation.

## 3. Related Work

Roughly twenty recent methods condition robot manipulation on human demonstration data. The single most useful axis for positioning this project is whether the human demonstration is **retained and consulted live at test time**, or **absorbed into policy weights during training and discarded**. A second, largely orthogonal axis is whether the method says anything at all about **where the robot should stand** — almost none do.

**3.1 Conditioning mechanisms in image generation — the core analogy.** ControlNet (arXiv 2302.05543) adds a new spatial condition (pose, depth, edges) to a frozen, pre-trained diffusion model via a trainable copy of the backbone joined by zero-initialized convolutions: at initialization the added branch contributes nothing, so training starts exactly from the base model's behavior, and the branch learns a conditional residual on top. This "frozen backbone + zero-init residual side-branch" pattern is the clearest structural analogy for what we want a VLA to do with a human demonstration, and it has already been adapted for robot policies: Diff-Control (IROS 2024) applies zero-convolution injection to a diffusion policy's action sequence, and ELAN4D attaches a zero-initialized residual branch to a VLA's action expert. These are the closest existing templates for a "conditioning branch on a frozen VLA" design.

**3.2 Training-time-only absorption — human demo discarded after training.** EMMA (arXiv 2509.04443, Georgia Tech) is the closest prior work in spirit: it uses Meta ARIA to capture human mobile-manipulation demonstrations and co-trains on heterogeneous human and robot data through per-embodiment stems, using human head-trajectory data to learn a base-movement head with no explicit region-invariant mechanism — the human demo is not available at deployment. EgoMimic (arXiv 2410.24221) co-trains a unified policy on human hand-pose and robot joint-action prediction using co-located, rig-aligned capture (masking human/robot pixels to close the appearance gap), and discards mobility entirely. HoMMI (arXiv 2603.03243) follows a similar co-located-rig recipe (a UMI gripper plus head-mounted iPhone) for whole-body mobile manipulation, again with no live demo at test time. EgoBridge (arXiv 2509.19626, NeurIPS 2025) aligns human and robot policy features via optimal transport shaped by DTW-matched "pseudo-pairs," used purely as an auxiliary training loss. X-Diffusion (arXiv 2511.04671) restricts human-demo supervision to diffusion noise levels above a learned human/robot indistinguishability threshold, so human data teaches only coarse intent, and only during training. GHOST (arXiv 2606.10025, RSS 2026) splits control into an embodiment-agnostic high-level sub-goal predictor (trained jointly on human and robot data) and an embodiment-specific low-level controller (robot-only); human video only ever touches the high-level module, and no live demo is consulted at deployment. EgoScale (arXiv 2602.16710, NVIDIA) pretrains a flow-based VLA on tens of thousands of hours of egocentric human video, then mid-trains on a much smaller aligned human+robot set, and post-trains on robot data — again fully training-time; notably, EgoScale explicitly fixes the robot's base and does not address positioning at all.

**3.3 Test-time-retained prompting — the family this project belongs to.** Vid2Robot (arXiv 2403.12943) encodes a human prompt video and the robot's current state into a shared representation via cross-attention, trained with temporal-cycle-consistency and contrastive losses on paired prompt-video/robot-trajectory data, and requires the prompt video at inference. UniSkill (arXiv 2505.08787, CoRL 2025) pretrains inverse/forward "skill dynamics" models on large-scale, fully unpaired cross-embodiment video, then extracts a skill latent from a human prompt video at test time to condition a robot policy — notably without needing frame-level pairing, though it leans on large-scale pretraining to get there. XSkill (arXiv 2307.09955) discovers cross-embodiment skill prototypes via SwAV/Sinkhorn-Knopp clustering and aligns a prompt video to the robot's current progress via a Skill Alignment Transformer at inference. Gen2Act (arXiv 2409.16283) sidesteps the need for a real paired human video by generating one zero-shot from an off-the-shelf video model, then extracting point tracks from the generated video to condition a closed-loop policy — the "human demo" consulted at test time is synthetic. VidBot (arXiv 2503.07135, CVPR 2025) retargets in-the-wild monocular human video into a metric-scale 3D affordance representation and deploys the extracted 3D action directly, without any policy co-training. DemoDiffusion (arXiv 2506.20668, ICRA 2026) retargets a single human demonstration's hand motion into a robot end-effector trajectory, then uses it as a partially-noised initialization for a frozen, pretrained diffusion policy's reverse sampling — the noise level trades off demonstration fidelity against the policy's own prior, recovering the base policy exactly in the high-noise limit. ICRT (Berkeley) treats full robot (not human) demonstration trajectories as raw in-context prompts for a causal transformer, with a "no-prompt-loss" trick that forces the model to actually use the prompt rather than ignore it.

Our proposal sits in this second family — closest in spirit to DemoDiffusion (retained single demo, pretrained backbone, no retraining of the base VLA) and UniSkill (a compact latent extracted from the human demo, embodiment-agnostic by construction) — but differs from all of them in scope: none of Vid2Robot, UniSkill, XSkill, Gen2Act, or DemoDiffusion address where the robot should stand; they all assume the robot is already correctly positioned.

**3.4 Retargeting-only pipelines — the path we explicitly avoid.** A separate line of work — HumanEgo (arXiv 2605.24934), VidBot, and Do-As-I-Do (arXiv 2606.19333, UC Berkeley) — retargets human hand/arm motion directly into robot action space via heuristic or learned mappings (e.g. index+thumb ≈ pincer grasp), typically requiring heavy off-the-shelf preprocessing (object segmentation, human inpainting) to make the resulting data trainable. HumanEgo specifically trains a flow-matching policy from ~30 minutes of egocentric human video per task, reporting strong success rates without any robot data — but the policy is trained on human video from scratch rather than conditioning an already-pretrained VLA. Because retargeted trajectories only replay demonstrated motion with no interaction feedback, a closed-loop policy with emergent recovery behavior is difficult to reach this way, and none of the three address mobile positioning — they all assume co-located or fixed-base capture. This is the key differentiator from our approach: instead of training a new policy on many retargeted human demonstrations, we use a single human demonstration to condition an already-pretrained VLA at test time.

**3.5 Structured/domain conditioning precedents for a "with and without" mechanism.** Since our own architecture needs to work both with and without a human demonstration present (Section 7), it is worth noting how other systems solve an analogous problem. π0.7 (arXiv 2604.15483, Physical Intelligence) trains one VLA under randomly-dropped combinations of structured context (subtask, subgoal image, episode metadata), giving a single policy that flexibly uses or ignores each optional signal — a direct, classifier-free-guidance-style precedent, and the closest template for how "Clever Conditioning" in Figure 1 might be trained to degrade gracefully without a human demo. GR-MG (arXiv 2408.14368) trains a goal-conditioned policy on a real future frame but deploys it on a goal image generated from language and progress, with graceful behavior when text is absent. X-VLA (arXiv 2510.10274) takes an orthogonal approach, assigning each robot/data domain a learned soft prompt that absorbs embodiment- and setup-specific variation, separate from task content — a reminder that "human demonstration" conditioning and "which embodiment/domain" conditioning are two different axes, relevant once we deploy on both Stretch and Galaxia.

**3.6 Positioning specifically is a comparatively open problem.** Almost all of the above work, on both sides of the test-time/training-time split, treats base placement as solved or out of scope. The one baseline that directly addresses "given a fixed manipulation policy, where should the robot stand" is Mobi-π (arXiv 2505.23692), which finds a working base pose via per-scene 3D Gaussian-splat reconstruction plus roughly 20 minutes of Bayesian optimization — a fundamentally more expensive procedure than reading an offset off a single human demonstration, and one that uses no human demonstration at all. This is where our human-centric offset formulation (Section 5) has the clearest, least-crowded case to make: the manipulation-conditioning space already has several worked examples (Vid2Robot, UniSkill, XSkill, Gen2Act, DemoDiffusion), whereas positioning-from-a-single-human-demo essentially does not.

**3.7 Test-time weight adaptation (TTT) — a third mechanism, and the two papers reviewed most recently.** Distinct from both training-time-only absorption (Section 3.2) and test-time-retained prompting-as-input (Section 3.3), a third mechanism absorbs the human demonstration into the model's *weights* at test time via gradient-based test-time training (TTT), rather than treating it as an additional token/context input to an otherwise-frozen network.

- *RoboTTT* (NVIDIA, Stanford, UT Austin, 2026 — research.nvidia.com/labs/gear/robottt) integrates TTT into a VLA/WAM backbone (GR00T-N1.7) by giving the policy a recurrent state made of "fast weights" — parameters updated by gradient descent on a self-supervised objective during both training and inference — scaling effective visuomotor context to 8,000 timesteps (~5 minutes at 30 Hz) without growing inference latency. At this context length it exhibits one-shot in-context imitation from a single human video demonstration (6/10 success vs. 0/10 for short-context baselines), on-the-fly policy improvement from the robot's own deployment history, and stronger performance on long-horizon, multi-stage tasks. It operates on a fixed base/tabletop setting throughout and does not address positioning.
- *WAM-TTT* (arXiv 2607.06988, Peking University, Galbot, CASIA, Tsinghua, 2026) is structurally the closest work to our own data plan. It meta-trains a lightweight key–value fast-weight memory using a small set of **paired human–robot demonstrations** (2,286 episodes across 9 tasks in their experiments), so human visual cues become predictive of robot behavior through the world-action model's shared video–action representation. At **deployment, only unlabeled human video is needed** — the pretrained WAM and all slow weights stay frozen, and the fast-weight memory is adapted purely by self-supervised video prediction on the human clip, with no robot data, hand-pose annotation, or action retargeting at test time. This is close to our own two-stage data plan (Section 8): a modest paired human+robot set to learn the alignment, then a human-only sample to condition at test time. Like RoboTTT, it operates on tabletop/bimanual manipulation across three embodiments (Unitree G1, Galbot gripper, Galbot Sharpa) and does not address base positioning.

Both papers reinforce the core hypothesis of Section 2 — that a frozen, pretrained foundation model can be steered by a human demonstration without full retraining — but implement conditioning through gradient-based weight adaptation rather than through architectural conditioning tokens or diffusion initialization, and neither predicts where the robot should stand. This gives a concrete additional "how" to weigh for our own conditioning mechanism (Section 7): TTT fast weights are a genuine alternative to the "Clever Conditioning" block in Figure 1, worth comparing against a plain conditioning-token or ControlNet-style residual approach. This is also the "Peking University / NVIDIA test-time-training line of work" flagged in the original discussion notes, now identified precisely as these two 2026 papers.

## 4. Problem Formulation

We assume the human and robot share a pre-built map, i.e. a common world frame $W$.

A human demonstration recorded with ARIA provides, for each frame $i$: egocentric RGB, a SLAM pose $T_{WC_i}$ (via re-localization), and wrist/hand motion $T_{C_1 H}$. This demonstration is encoded into a compact latent $z$ that captures task intent, approach direction, and contact intent, together with a mode embedding $T \in \{\text{Positioning}, \text{Manipulation}\}$ that determines which behavior the shared policy should produce.

We decompose mobile manipulation into two modes handled by **one policy**:

- **Positioning.** A multi-task policy predicts a human-centric base offset $T_{C_1 B} = \pi_\theta(o_t, z, T{=}\text{Positioning})$ — i.e. where the robot should stand *relative to where the human stood*, not an absolute world location (this can run closed-loop but at low frequency). Composing this offset with the human's localized pose gives the robot's target base pose in the world frame, $T_{WB} = T_{WC_1} \cdot T_{C_1B}$, which is sent to Nav2 to drive the robot there.
- **Manipulation.** Once at $B$, the same policy, conditioned on the same $z$, continually infers the target end-effector pose $T^t_{BG} = \pi_\theta(o_t, z, T{=}\text{Manipulation})$ from live observation. An inverse-kinematics (IK) solver converts $T^t_{BG}$ into joint commands at each step, closing the control loop.

Net effect: one human demonstration → one latent $z$ → one policy that drives both *where to stand* and *how to act*, keeping the two stages coherent rather than independently learned.

## 5. Mobile Manipulation Approach

We define mobile manipulation in two clearly separated phases: first navigate the robot to a suitable position near the object (mobility), then perform manipulation with the base fixed (manipulation — the arm acts without further local base movement).

- **Positioning via human-centric offsets.** Rather than predicting world-frame coordinates, the policy predicts an offset relative to the human demonstration's pose. This is designed to improve generalization across environments, since the correction does not depend on where the object sits in the map.
- **A known failure mode this addresses.** Policies such as $\pi_0$ have been observed to navigate to the wrong side of an object (e.g. the wrong side of a fridge). A policy that predicts correct human-centric positioning can, in principle, always succeed on this class of task.
- **Base movement during manipulation** can be solved via IK (from a URDF) applied to gripper actions rather than learned — consistent with prior Stretch work, where base and arm form a single kinematic chain.
- **Semantically-aware base movement** (e.g. approaching a fridge from the left vs. the right depending on hinge side or obstruction) does require learned conditioning beyond pure IK — this is exactly the role of $z$ in the Positioning mode.
- **Navigation to the vicinity** is handled by ROS2/Nav2 once a human-centric goal pose is predicted; no map-based conditioning is needed inside the VLA itself.

## 7. Conditioning Mechanism

**Goal.** Let a single human demonstration steer a pretrained video-generation backbone (Cosmos 3) directly, so that the resulting representation conditions the robot policy — without training a separate alignment encoder from scratch.

**Motivation.** An earlier version of this design trained a dedicated latent-alignment encoder (dual reconstruction heads + SIGReg) before any conditioning could happen at all. That approach has been abandoned in favor of directly reusing Cosmos 3's own pretrained video-generation prior, following two recent papers:

- **mimic-video** (arXiv 2512.15692): couples a pretrained video diffusion backbone with a lightweight flow-matching action decoder (an Inverse Dynamics Model, IDM) that conditions on the video backbone's intermediate latent representations, rather than on fully decoded pixels. Stopping the video model's denoising early (a single forward pass, flow time τv ≈ 1) yields *better* downstream action prediction than full pixel reconstruction, and yields roughly 10x greater sample efficiency than a comparable VLM/VLA-conditioned decoder.
- **Masked Visual Actions (MVA)** (arXiv 2607.19343): expresses "action" as a partially revealed trajectory of an entity inside a video. Revealing an object's desired motion lets the same video model recover the robot behavior consistent with that outcome — an inverse-modeling trick that avoids hand-to-gripper retargeting entirely, since object motion (not human hand/wrist motion) is the shared, embodiment-agnostic conditioning signal.

Combining these two ideas lets Cosmos 3 (already the selected backbone, with an identified injection point) absorb the human-demonstration conditioning role directly, in place of a dedicated alignment encoder.

### 7.1 Architecture

The architecture (Figure 2 in the shared Word doc): human demo video, the mode token T, and — in manipulation mode — the robot's current frame all feed a mode-conditioned Cosmos 3 in a single forward pass; the resulting latent feeds a shared IDM trunk, which splits into two mode-specific output heads (positioning, manipulation), as discussed in Section 7.2.

### 7.2 Mechanism

Getting Cosmos 3 ready for this role requires **two finetuning stages, split by training objective, not by data source**:

- **Stage A — finetune Cosmos 3 into a human-demo-prompt-conditioned video generator (video-reconstruction loss).** This is the core finetuning pass: Cosmos 3 is LoRA-finetuned so that, conditioned on human demo video (playing the same role language instruction plays in the original Cosmos 3) plus the mode token T ∈ {Positioning, Manipulation} (Section 4) — and, for manipulation mode, the robot's current frame as well — it generates the correct future robot frame or rollout. Training uses the **paired human+robot data** (Section 8, ~100–200 pairs): the loss compares generated frames/latents against the ground-truth robot rollout that actually followed the paired human demo. This single pass does double duty — it teaches Cosmos 3 both to use human demo video as a conditioning prompt *and* to generate correctly in this project's specific robot/lab visual domain (the domain-adaptation role previously assigned to a separate stage). The **robot-only demo pool** (~10–20 per task, Section 8) can be mixed in as supplementary unconditioned/self-conditioned video for additional domain coverage, but the paired data is what actually teaches the human-demo-conditioning behavior. Cosmos 3's weights are frozen after this stage.
- **Stage B — train a shared IDM (action-regression loss, generator frozen).** With Stage A's finetuned Cosmos 3 completely frozen, train a **single shared IDM trunk with two mode-specific output heads**, rather than two fully independent networks — this keeps the "one demo → one z → one policy" principle of Section 4 intact at the read-out stage too, and is more parameter- and sample-efficient given the ~100–200 paired demonstrations available. This stage never touches Cosmos 3's weights, only the shared trunk and its two output heads, and uses the same paired data:

- **Manipulation mode.** Cosmos 3 (Stage A) predicts a near, continuous multi-frame rollout (mirroring mimic-video's partial-denoising strategy: a single forward pass at τv ≈ 1 is sufficient), conditioned on human video latent *plus* the robot's current frame. The shared IDM trunk reads the resulting intermediate-layer latent **and the robot's current frame as a current-observation input** (not only through Cosmos 3's conditioning) — mirroring mimic-video's action decoder, which cross-attends to the video latent while separately encoding the robot's own proprioceptive state — and the manipulation output head regresses wrist-pose-delta + gripper closure.
- **Positioning mode.** Cosmos 3 (Stage A) predicts a distant, single target frame — "what the robot would observe once correctly positioned" — which is a target-frame generation problem in the spirit of GR-MG (Section 3.5) rather than a continuous rollout. The condition is human video latent **only**; the robot's current-observation input slot in the shared IDM trunk is filled with a learned mask/null token rather than the robot's actual current frame, since it may share no visual overlap with the human demo's location, and letting the trunk see it here would ask the model to relate temporal continuity between two unrelated scenes it was never trained to relate. The positioning output head then regresses the offset from the trunk's output.

**Why a shared trunk needs an explicit mask, not just an absent input.** Both modes must present the IDM trunk with the *same* input signature — a current-frame slot that is either the real robot frame (manipulation) or a null token (positioning) — because a shared network needs a fixed-shape input regardless of mode, and this null-token pattern is the same structured-conditioning-with-dropout precedent already cited in Section 3.5 for π0.5. The mode token T tells the trunk which case it's in and which output head to activate.

**Give the trunk mode T explicitly, not just implicitly.** As designed so far, the trunk only infers which mode it is in indirectly — from whether the current-frame slot is real or masked, and from the statistical differences between a rollout latent and a target-frame latent. It is cheap and worth doing to instead feed mode T into the trunk as an explicit conditioning signal (e.g. FiLM-style modulation of the trunk's hidden layers) — FiLM was rejected earlier (Section 7, historical note) only for conditioning Cosmos 3 itself, since a fixed modulation mechanism doesn't transfer across backbones; that objection does not apply to the IDM trunk, which is a small network built and fully controlled by this project. An explicit mode signal gives the trunk a clean, always-available switch, making it easier to learn a genuine internal separation between the two modes' computation rather than having to infer the mode from incidental input statistics.

This is a real improvement, but it addresses a **different** problem than the mask-leakage risk above, and does not resolve that risk on its own: mask leakage is about the two modes sharing the same trunk *parameters*, so that gradients from manipulation-mode training can still shape the parameters positioning-mode inference relies on, however clearly the trunk is told which mode it's currently in. Explicit mode conditioning makes mode-switching more robust; it does not guarantee parameter-level isolation between the two modes. The shared-trunk-vs-two-independent-IDMs ablation described above remains the right way to measure whether that isolation is actually good enough in practice, and should be run regardless of whether explicit mode conditioning is added.

**Risk to watch: mask leakage.** If some paired training episodes happen to have partial visual overlap between the human demo's location and the robot's current frame, the shared trunk could learn to quietly exploit the current-frame slot even in positioning mode, despite the mask being present at the architecture level — a shortcut that would fail exactly when the two locations have no overlap at all, which is the common case at deployment. This is a real risk that two fully separate IDMs (the earlier design) did not have, since a positioning-only network never saw the robot's current frame during training at all. **This should be run as an explicit ablation** — shared-trunk-with-masking vs. two fully independent IDMs — comparing (a) data efficiency and (b) positioning robustness specifically on paired episodes with no visual overlap, rather than assumed away.

**Critical design choice, for both modes: no explicit pixel decoding, no visual odometry.** Recovering an offset by decoding the predicted target frame to pixels and then estimating relative pose via visual odometry (VIO) would introduce scale ambiguity (monocular two-view relative pose is only determined up to scale without depth or known-scale cues) and would stack a second source of error (pose-estimation error) on top of the generation error itself. Instead, **the shared IDM trunk reads the frozen intermediate-layer latent directly, and each mode-specific output head regresses its target end-to-end** — the positioning head regresses the human-centric offset T_C1B, the manipulation head regresses the action — with metric scale learned implicitly from the ARIA SLAM ground truth used during training, exactly as mimic-video's action IDM does for actions. This keeps the two modes methodologically symmetric and keeps generalization resting on the learned mapping rather than on a brittle geometric reconstruction step.

The offset T_C1B is then composed with the human's localized pose via the same purely geometric step already defined in Section 4 ($T_{WB} = T_{WC_1} \cdot T_{C_1B}$) and sent to Nav2; no learning happens in this last step.

### 7.3 Signals available from ARIA

SLAM poses, hand tracking, and potentially whole-body human reconstruction, which could provide a richer conditioning signal than end-effector pose alone, and can be layered into the human video condition without changing the mechanism above.

### 7.4 Data requirements

This mechanism reuses two data pools already budgeted in Section 8, but assigns them roles aligned with the corrected Stage A/B split:

- **Paired human+robot demos (~100–200 total, Section 8)** are the primary corpus for **Stage A** — they are what actually teaches Cosmos 3 to condition on human demo video, since Stage A's loss needs the ground-truth robot rollout that followed each paired human demo. The same pool then serves **Stage B**, training the shared IDM trunk and its two output heads. Positioning's target-frame pairs (human observation at position A ↔ robot observation at the corresponding correct position B) can be extracted from these same paired episodes — no separate data collection is required.
- **Robot-only demos (~10–20 per task, Section 8)** now serve a supplementary role in Stage A only — additional unconditioned/self-conditioned video to broaden Cosmos 3's coverage of this project's specific visual domain — in addition to their original role anchoring embodiment for manipulation. They play no role in Stage B, since Stage B needs paired ground truth to regress against.

Both the positioning and manipulation output heads require paired supervision to learn what offset or action is actually correct — this mechanism does not add new paired-data cost beyond what Section 8 already budgets, but it does fold the previously separate "domain adaptation" and "conditioning" objectives into a single Stage A finetuning pass, which a from-scratch alignment encoder would not have needed (a from-scratch encoder has no pretrained video prior to adapt in the first place).

### 7.5 Relation to existing related work

- Manipulation branch: directly combines mimic-video (Section 3.3 family) and MVA — both very recent (Dec 2025 / Jul 2026) and not yet cited in Section 3; should be added there as mechanism 4, alongside TTT (Section 3.7).
- Positioning branch: extends the GR-MG-style target-image generation idea (already cited in Section 3.5) to the positioning problem for the first time — Section 3.6's claim that positioning-from-a-single-human-demo is largely unaddressed still holds and is strengthened by this connection.
- Risk to flag directly: mimic robotics (mimic-video's authors) have already productized a closely related idea as FLUX-mimic, a commercial partnership with Black Forest Labs (FLUX 3), announced July 23, 2026. FLUX 3's Action capability is closed, early-access-only, and not a feasible backbone choice right now (no access to internal weights or intermediate layers). Cosmos 3 remains the right backbone for this reason alone, in addition to the existing engineering investment (source access, identified injection point). This also means the project's differentiation claim should be anchored specifically on **cross-embodiment human-demo conditioning** and **positioning**, since a general "video-action model for robot control" claim alone is no longer uncontested.

### 7.6 Generalization hypothesis

Conditioning learned on one articulated-door task (e.g. opening a fridge or cabinet) should transfer to a related task (e.g. opening a microwave), since the underlying motion primitive is similar and only the contact point varies. This is a testable prediction of the approach and a good candidate for an early generalization experiment.

| Data type | Purpose | Rough quantity estimate |
|---|---|---|
| Paired human + robot demos, per task/object category (e.g. fridge, cabinet, microwave, drawer) | Learn the latent-alignment / conditioning mechanism (Section 7) | ~30–50 paired demos per category to start; 3–4 categories → ~100–200 paired demos total |
| Robot-only demos, per task | Anchor embodiment correctly (avoid the retargeting fragility seen in HumanEgo/VidBot) | ~10–20 per task |
| Human-only demos (ARIA), diverse environments/objects, per task category | Provide the bulk of the generalization signal, cheaply collected | 100+ per category, opportunistically expandable via passive multi-hour ARIA sessions |
| Test-time demo | The actual conditioning signal at deployment — a single new-task demonstration, not seen during training | 1 demonstration per new task/environment |

These are first-pass estimates and should be revisited once the latent-alignment experiment (Section 7) gives a sense of how much paired data is actually needed to get clean alignment. No massive pre-training dataset is required, since we deliberately leverage an already pre-trained VLA rather than training a manipulation policy from scratch.

**Both the positioning and manipulation heads now rely on the same paired human+robot pool.** An earlier version of this design let the positioning head train on unpaired human-only and robot-only data, since its underlying representation-learning objective (relative-SE(3) reconstruction) required no cross-modal translation. That objective belonged to the now-abandoned alignment-encoder approach (see Section 7). Under the current video-generation-based mechanism, positioning's target-frame pairs (human observation at position A ↔ robot observation at the correct position B) must come from paired episodes, so the human-only and robot-only pools above now serve mainly to (a) provide diverse conditioning inputs and (b) supplement Cosmos 3's Stage A finetuning with extra domain coverage (Section 7.2), rather than to train a separate unpaired positioning representation.

## 9. Addressing Potential Concerns

**"This positioning policy can hardly generalize to a new environment."** We predict a human-centric offset, not an absolute pose, so the correction is region-invariant — it does not depend on where the object sits in the map. This is also where test-time human conditioning shows its clearest measurable value: positioning may show the largest empirical lift from $z$ in ablations, even though positioning and manipulation are architecturally equal, first-class consumers of the same shared policy and latent.

**"Over-emphasis on positioning rather than mobile manipulation."** Architecturally, positioning and manipulation are equally important — same $z$, same shared policy. Positioning is comparatively under-explored in prior learn-from-human work, which assumes co-located or rig-aligned capture, or operates in place from a fixed base. Even work that does use ARIA for mobile manipulation (e.g. EMMA) trains an end-to-end navigation policy, inheriting the same generalization problem, since it has no region-invariant mechanism.

**"Why do we need a positioning policy at all, given an orchestrator system?"** The figure below illustrates the reasoning. Most existing approaches only cover a narrow positioning region around a fixed demo region, so out-of-distribution placements fail. Simply scaling up demo data (as very large VLAs like the $\pi$ series attempt) expands the covered region but still requires proportionally more data. Our approach instead uses the broad, cheaply-collected "human region" (from passive ARIA capture) to expand coverage around the narrower positioning region needed at deployment, without requiring that much data be robot-specific.

![Figure 3: Potential Concerns — "Why do we need a positioning policy even though there is an orchestrator system?" *Figure 3. Region comparison, as discussed in Section 9.*

**"Why not follow the HumanEgo-style path directly?"** HumanEgo and VidBot are retargeting pipelines that need heavy off-the-shelf preprocessing (object-centric segmentation, human inpainting) just to make an object-centric policy trainable, and they train on human demonstrations recorded for speed/motion alignment rather than natural task execution. Because retargeted trajectories only replay demonstrated motion with no interaction feedback, a closed-loop policy with emergent recovery behavior is essentially unreachable this way, and coupling a second regression target (base movement) on top only compounds the fragility from the larger embodiment gap. We instead follow the more established recipe: a few robot demonstrations to anchor the embodiment correctly, plus a large, diverse set of human demonstrations for generalization.

## 10. Expected Results and Evaluation

- **Quantitative.** A table comparing VLA baseline success rate vs. VLA + human-conditioning success rate across a set of tasks, showing consistent improvement from conditioning.
- **Hypothesis 2 ceiling ablation.** Alongside the no-conditioning floor and human-demo-conditioned result above, report VLA + robot-own-demo-conditioning (same task category, a different instance than the test episode) as an oracle-adjacent ceiling. The gap between human-conditioned and robot-conditioned performance is a direct measure of how much of the cross-embodiment "translation tax" the video-generation conditioning mechanism (Section 7) manages to recover, separating failures of conditioning quality from failures of the underlying video backbone's manipulation competence.
- **Qualitative.** Real-world demonstrations of intricate tasks (e.g. operating a kettle with a hidden button, opening a microwave) where the robot fails without conditioning but succeeds once given a single human demonstration.
- **Stretch goal.** Demonstrating the approach on two robot embodiments (Stretch and Galaxia) would substantially strengthen the argument that the conditioning mechanism, not embodiment-specific tuning, is doing the work.
- Because the approach is learned from human demonstrations and there is currently no simulation support for this setting, real-robot evaluation is necessary; results in simulation alone would not be convincing.
